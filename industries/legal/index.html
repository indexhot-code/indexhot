Industry guide · Legal

AI search visibility for legal services

Legal queries are local and credential-driven. When someone asks an assistant for a lawyer, the answer is assembled from firm pages, directory profiles and bar association records — so consistent entity data across all three matters more than another explanatory article.

Local intent, national-scale models

Most legal searches are constrained by jurisdiction and location, which makes entity matching the core problem. The model has to know that your firm practises in a specific area, in a specific place, in a specific field, and that a real, licensed person stands behind the page.

  • Practice-area clarity. One page per practice area beats a single “services” page.
  • Jurisdiction signals. State, city and court coverage stated in text.
  • Credential corroboration. Bar admissions and directory listings that match your site.

Common visibility gaps

  • Attorney bios without practice areas, jurisdictions or admissions.
  • Office locations published only as embedded maps.
  • No FAQ content answering fee structure, timelines or process.
  • Inconsistent firm naming across directories and the site.

Legal action plan

  1. Build one page per practice area with jurisdiction stated in the first paragraph.
  2. Give every attorney a profile with admissions, experience and contact details.
  3. Publish office addresses and service areas as text, not map embeds.
  4. Add LegalService and Attorney structured data.
  5. Answer fee and process questions directly — these are high-intent prompts.

What the data shows

Legal queries are the most local and the most source-conservative in our sample. Answers cite statutes, bar associations and court materials, and they name firms mainly when the question includes a jurisdiction. A firm without location and practice-area structure in its markup is effectively invisible for the queries that matter, because the answer cannot tell which jurisdiction the page serves.

How to verify it worked

Run five questions that include your city and practice area, then five that do not. The second set usually shows near-zero presence, which is expected and not a failure. For the first set, check whether the answer names your firm and whether it cites your page for a procedural or eligibility claim. Publishing one structured practice-area page per jurisdiction, with explicit eligibility rules, typically moves the local set within a month.

The questions buyers actually ask

These five prompts are a usable starting library for legal services buyers. Ask them verbatim across all four surfaces and record mention, citation and absorption per prompt before changing anything.

  • Do I need a lawyer for this type of contract in this state?
  • What is the filing deadline for this claim in this jurisdiction?
  • How much does an uncontested matter typically cost here?
  • Which court handles this type of dispute?
  • What documents are required to start this process?

Where teams get stuck

Firms usually get stuck because their site is organised by practice area while questions are organised by jurisdiction and procedure. Eligibility rules and deadlines sit inside PDFs and intake forms, so answers cite statutes instead. Adding structured per-jurisdiction pages is tedious but it is the only route that matches how the questions are actually asked.

Frequently asked questions

Why do law firms struggle to appear in AI answers?

Usually because practice area, jurisdiction and credentials are not stated as extractable facts. Models need to match a firm to a place, a specialty and a licensed person.

Do online directories affect legal AI visibility?

Yes. Directory and bar association profiles corroborate your entity data, and models use that corroboration when choosing which firm to name.

Should each practice area have its own page?

Yes. Separate pages give the model a clean match between query intent and service, while a single generic services page provides nothing specific to ground on.
