The GEO Measurement Playbook

By IndexHot Team · November 26, 2026 · 9 sections

A measurement is only useful if somebody else can repeat it. This playbook turns the IndexHot standard into an operating procedure: fifty prompts split ten and forty, three outcomes coded separately, three runs per surface, and a four-level rubric for the one metric that cannot be automated. It takes about three hours per cycle and produces a number you can defend, plus a content brief you can act on.

Why measurement comes first

Most GEO programmes start with content because content feels like progress. They then discover that they cannot tell whether anything worked, because there was no baseline and no frozen question set.

Measurement first is not a preference, it is the only way to attribute anything later. If you fix access and rewrite pages in the same month, a change in visibility could be either fix or neither, and you will spend the next quarter arguing about it.

It is also cheap. A first cycle costs about three hours once the library exists. That is less than the cost of one badly briefed article, and it decides whether the next ten articles are aimed at the right questions.

Step 1: build the fifty-prompt library

The split is ten branded and forty non-branded, and the ratio matters more than the total. Branded prompts test what the model already believes about you; non-branded prompts test whether it would ever introduce you. A library of only branded prompts measures recognition, not acquisition.

The forty are distributed across eight intent clusters, because each cluster fails differently and therefore needs a different fix.

Cluster Prompts What it tests
Category discovery 5 Whether you exist in the consideration set at all
Comparison 5 Whether you appear when a choice is being made
Pricing and budget 5 Whether your commercial facts are retrievable
Implementation 5 Whether your operational detail is retrievable
Problem and symptom 5 Whether you are surfaced to buyers already in motion
Alternatives and switching 5 Whether you appear in displacement queries
Trust and risk 5 Whether you can carry the reassurance half of a decision
Fit and segment 5 Whether you are matched to a specific buyer shape

Write the prompts in the buyer's phrasing, not yours. Pull the wording from sales calls, support tickets and search queries, then check each one reads like something a person would actually type. Internal jargon produces a library that measures your vocabulary.

Freeze it, version it, and do not touch it mid-cycle. Rewording a prompt between cycles produces a trend that describes your edits rather than the market, and the failure is undetectable afterwards unless the original wording was recorded.

Fifty is the right number for manual work. Below thirty, single-prompt noise passes for a trend. Above a hundred, marginal precision improves but the discipline of re-running monthly collapses, and discipline is worth more than precision here.

Step 2: define the three outcomes

Three outcomes, three columns, no blending. The definitions must be written down before the first run, because the moment you start scoring a long sample the standard quietly relaxes.

Mention means the brand name appears in the answer text. Nothing more. A brand named in passing counts, and a brand described at length without being named does not.

Citation means a domain you control is attributed as a source, whether by link, footnote or named reference. A third-party page about you is a mention of your brand but not a citation of your site; keep them apart, because the fixes differ.

Absorption means a distinctive claim of yours appears in the answer in substance. This is the only one that requires judgement, which is why step four exists.

The reason to separate them is diagnostic. Mention without citation points to structure or freshness. Citation without mention is possible but unusual and usually indicates a source list attached without the brand being discussed. Mention and citation without absorption points to generic content.

Step 3: run the protocol

Each prompt is run three times per surface, and the median answer is what gets coded. Single runs are samples: a prompt that names you twice and ignores you once has an unsettled answer, and the honest record is "unsettled" rather than an average.

Log four fields per row: prompt id, surface, run date, and model or version identifier where the interface exposes one. Six months later, when somebody asks why a number moved, those fields are the difference between an explanation and a guess.

Fix the account state. Logged-in and logged-out answers differ because personalisation and model version differ, so run every cycle from the same state. Turning personalisation off where the interface allows it reduces one source of variance at no cost.

One cycle for one brand across four surfaces takes about three hours. A monthly cadence is the shortest interval worth reporting: daily checks measure the engine's variance rather than your content, and teams that check daily spend their weeks reacting to noise they cannot influence.

Step 4: code absorption

Four levels, scored at the highest reached anywhere in the answer rather than averaged across sentences.

Level Label What it requires
0 None The domain is attached, but nothing from the page appears beyond the name
1 Topical A theme associated with your page appears, but any competitor could have supplied it
2 Claim A specific checkable claim of yours appears in substance: a figure, threshold, condition or date
3 Recommendation The answer adopts your positioning or names you for a defined use case

Before scoring, write down five to eight claims from the source page that are distinctive enough that a competitor could not plausibly supply them. Those become the anchor list for the whole sample, and they prevent the coding from drifting toward whatever the analyst remembers.

Calibrate two coders on twenty answers before scoring the bulk. Where they disagree by more than a level, fix the rubric with an example rather than an argument. A rubric with edge cases is usable; a rubric that depends on who is coding is not.

The level-1 to level-2 boundary is where reporting gets inflated. Level 1 feels like success in a sheet and represents almost no influence. If your absorption rate looks strong, check what proportion of the sample sits exactly at level 1.

Step 5: compute and band

The score is 100 × (0.30 · MR + 0.30 · CR + 0.40 · AR), with each rate expressed as a fraction of prompts run. Absorption carries the largest weight because it is the only component that measures influence rather than presence.

Four bands describe what a score implies for a buyer rather than how much work has been done: 0 to 25 invisible, 26 to 55 mentioned, 56 to 80 cited, 81 to 100 recommended. The middle two are where most brands sit, and the most useful distinction inside them is the gap between citation and absorption.

For a cross-platform figure, score each surface separately and average with explicit weights — our default is ChatGPT 0.40, Perplexity 0.30 and Gemini 0.30. Never average the raw rates across surfaces first: a mention on one surface and a citation on another are different achievements that cancel out when blended.

Where a fourth surface matters commercially, add it as a named weight and record the change. A silently reweighted composite is indistinguishable from a methodology change when someone reads last quarter's number.

Step 6: report three numbers and one change

A report that a decision-maker can use has four parts: the three rates per surface, a comparison against two or three named competitors from the same run, the single change made since the last report, and the evidence that it worked or did not.

Put absorption first in the visual order. It is the number that predicts persuasion, and it is the one most likely to be missing from a vendor's dashboard because it cannot be automated cleanly.

Report cluster-level results, not only the aggregate. A line saying share of voice rose four points hides that you lost every implementation question while winning the comparisons. Cluster-level reporting is what converts a number into a brief.

Be explicit about what the measurement cannot show. Nothing here attributes pipeline or revenue, because no available measurement does. Claiming otherwise invites the audit that ends the conversation, and the honest chain — citations on owned pages, branded search lift, referral traffic where exposed — is persuasive enough on its own.

Six ways this goes wrong

  1. Prompt drift. The library gets quietly reworded between cycles, and the trend line describes the analyst's edits.
  2. Branded-only libraries. Recognition is measured, acquisition is not, and every number looks healthy while the non-branded gap stays invisible.
  3. Blended scoring. One composite hides which of the three metrics moved, so it cannot produce a next action.
  4. Single runs. One sample per prompt per cycle, reported as a result rather than as a sample.
  5. Uncalibrated absorption. One analyst, no anchor list, standard relaxing as fatigue sets in.
  6. Missing run records. No date or model version attached to rows, which makes every historical comparison unfalsifiable.

The pattern is that each failure is invisible at the time and expensive later. All six are prevented by the same two habits: write the definitions down before the first run, and date every row.

From measurement to a content brief

The measurement is only worth its cost if it produces a brief. The translation is mechanical, which is the point.

Start with the cluster where you are mentioned but not cited. That gap points to structure or freshness rather than reach, so the brief is a restructure: convert the relevant pages into question-shaped sections, move each claim into the opening sentence, and publish tables and specifications as text.

Then take the cluster where you are absent entirely and check access before writing anything. If pages in that cluster cannot be fetched or are not indexed, the answer is a technical brief, and no amount of content will substitute.

Finally, take the cluster where you are cited but not absorbed. That is a specificity problem: the pages are usable but generic. The brief there is to add one distinctive, checkable claim per section — a figure, a threshold, a dated outcome — rather than to expand the page.

Each brief names the cluster, the prompts, the pages involved and the single change expected. That shape makes it possible to check later whether the change worked, which is what closes the loop back into step three.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many prompts do I need for a reliable baseline?

Fifty: ten branded and forty non-branded across eight intent clusters. Below thirty, prompt-level noise dominates; above a hundred, the monthly cadence usually breaks before the precision pays off.

Can one person do the absorption coding?

They can, but the standard will drift over a long sample. The workable minimum is a written rubric, an anchor list of distinctive claims, and a twenty-answer calibration set scored twice.

How often should I re-run the cycle?

Monthly for trend, weekly only during an active change. Daily measurement produces noise that invites premature conclusions; quarterly misses the effect of work already paid for.

What if my numbers came from different tools across cycles?

Then the comparison is invalid unless the prompt library and definitions were identical. Switching tools mid-programme resets the baseline, and that reset should be recorded rather than hidden.

Does a good score mean buyers will convert?

No. It measures presence and influence inside answers, not demand or intent. Conversion depends on the offer behind the recommendation, which is a different question.
