The 12-Point GEO Audit We Run Before Any Content Work

By IndexHot Team · November 2, 2026

We run a twelve-point audit before writing anything, because most cases of AI invisibility are not content problems. In the audits we have done, crawl and entity issues account for more of the gap than thin content does, and fixing them first changes what the content work even needs to be. The audit takes roughly a day and prevents weeks of editing the wrong layer.

Why audit before content

Content work is expensive and slow to show up in measurement. Technical and entity fixes are cheap and often visible within weeks. Doing both at once makes it impossible to tell which produced the change, and makes it easy to conclude that content does not work when the real blocker was a robots rule.

The audit is also a calibration exercise. It tells you whether your benchmark should be a competitor with 400 pages or with 4,000, and whether your category is citation-concentrated or fragmented. Without that context, a score of 30 out of 100 means nothing.

The four clusters

Technical covers whether the page can be fetched, indexed and read. Entity covers whether a system can identify the brand at all. Measurement covers whether you can reproduce a result. Content covers whether there is anything worth citing.

Each cluster has a different owner in most organisations, which is why audits that stay inside one cluster tend to miss the actual constraint. A marketing team optimising copy cannot fix a noindex tag, and an engineering team fixing crawl cannot invent a distinctive claim.

The ordering matters because the clusters gate each other: technical failures make entity work invisible, entity ambiguity makes measurement unreliable, and unreliable measurement makes content decisions guesswork.

The twelve checks

Technical: (1) fetch key pages with a plain HTTP client and confirm body text is present; (2) check robots.txt against each named AI user-agent; (3) verify CDN or WAF does not challenge bot traffic; (4) confirm one canonical per page, absolute and self-referencing; (5) check that new pages have at least three inbound internal links.

Entity: (6) confirm one display name and one legal name used consistently on-site; (7) verify Organization markup with an absolute logo and live sameAs profiles; (8) state the parent-to-product relationship in text, not only in markup.

Measurement: (9) freeze and version a prompt library of fifty, split ten branded and forty non-branded; (10) record every run with date, platform and model version.

Content: (11) check that each key claim survives being copied out of the page alone; (12) confirm that the pages actually cited in your category are formats you also publish.

Scoring and prioritising

Score each check as pass, partial or fail, then order the failures by blast radius rather than by ease. A robots rule blocking one crawler affects every page at once; a missing FAQ block affects one page. Blast radius first, effort second.

In practice, the first three or four fixes are usually technical, and they change the measurement baseline. Re-run the baseline before starting content work, or you will later be unable to separate the effect of the fixes from the effect of the writing.

Anything that cannot be scored reproducibly should be dropped from the audit. A check that two analysts score differently is not a check, it is an opinion with a number attached.

What to do with the results

If technical checks fail, do not brief content yet. Fix access and canonicalisation, wait two to four weeks, and re-measure. Publishing into a broken crawl wastes both the content and the measurement.

If technical passes and entity is ambiguous, the work is naming and corroboration: consolidate the name, publish the markup, correct the directories. This is usually faster than content and higher leverage, because ambiguity caps every downstream metric.

Only when the first two clusters pass does content become the constraint, and at that point the audit tells you which format is missing: specification tables, comparisons, or dated policy pages. That is a far narrower brief than 'write more'.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does the audit take?

About a day for a small site and two to three days for one with a few hundred pages, mostly spent on the fetch tests, robots review and the directory comparison.

Can I run this without a technical team?

Checks nine through twelve and most of the entity cluster can be done by a marketer with a browser and a text editor. Checks one to five usually need someone who can query server logs or adjust CDN rules.

How often should the audit be repeated?

Quarterly, and always after a migration, a rebrand or a platform change. The audit is cheap to repeat and the failure modes are the ones that recur silently.
