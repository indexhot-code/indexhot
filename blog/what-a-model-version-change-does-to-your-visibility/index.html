What a Model Version Change Does to Your Visibility Numbers

By IndexHot Team · November 27, 2026

Every few weeks a provider ships a new model build and your dashboard moves. Nothing on your site changed. This post is about telling that kind of movement apart from a real regression, and about the record keeping that makes the difference visible at all.

Why the numbers move when the content does not

An answer is a sample from a model, a retrieval index and a prompt set. Change any of the three and the sample changes. Model updates alter how aggressively sources are retrieved, how long answers get, how often citations are attached and how willing the model is to name a specific brand rather than describe a category.

None of that is random noise. It is a different sampling distribution, which matters because teams routinely treat a distribution shift as evidence about their own work. A rise after a model update is not proof the rewrite helped; a fall is not proof it hurt.

The three things to record, every run

  1. Provider and model identifier, as the product reports it, plus the date of the run.
  2. The surface, because the same provider behaves differently inside a chat product and inside a summarised search result.
  3. Locale and whether you used a logged-in account, since personalisation and settings change what a fresh visitor sees.

Without those three, a comparison between two runs is a comparison between two things you cannot name. The most common reporting failure in this field is a quarter-over-quarter chart with no version annotation, which is unfalsifiable in both directions.

Separating drift from a real change

Run the same frozen prompt set twice in the same week, on the same version. The difference between those two runs is your noise floor. If your quarterly change is smaller than the noise floor, you have not measured anything yet.

What to tell stakeholders

Say which version produced the number, when it was measured, and what the noise floor was. Three sentences. It prevents the meeting where a marketing lead claims credit for a model release and the following one where they are blamed for it.

If a re-baseline is needed, say so plainly and reset expectations: the old number and the new number are not comparable, and pretending otherwise costs more credibility than the reset.

The habit that makes this manageable

Keep one sheet with a row per run: date, version, surface, prompt count, mention rate, citation rate, absorption rate, and a note field. Two years of that sheet is worth more than any single report, because it is the only artefact that can distinguish a trend from a series of unrelated shocks.

And date your own content changes in the same sheet. Without that, you cannot separate your edits from their releases, and the argument becomes unresolvable.
