A GEO report a board can actually read

By IndexHot Team · November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Most AI visibility reports fail in the same way: they present a composite score with a trend line, and nobody in the room can tell what changed or what to do. This is the structure we use instead, and it takes one page.

Four parts, in this order

Part Content Why it comes there
1. Rates Mention, citation and absorption per surface Three separate facts, because they fail differently
2. Comparison Same run, two or three named competitors Answers the only question the room actually asks
3. The change One thing changed since last report, and its evidence Prevents a list of activity from passing as progress
4. Next action One cluster, one page set, one expected effect Turns the number into work someone owns

The order matters. Leading with a composite invites a discussion about the score; leading with three rates invites a discussion about which one is failing.

Put absorption first in the visual order

It is the only rate that predicts persuasion, and it is the one most likely to be missing from a vendor dashboard because it requires judgement rather than scraping.

A report showing 70 percent mention, 35 percent citation and 12 percent absorption tells a clear story: the pipeline reaches the brand and uses it as evidence but keeps none of its specificity. That points at content, not at access, and the room can act on it.

Reversing the order, with mention first, produces congratulation followed by nothing. Teams report in the order that makes them look good and then wonder why nothing gets funded.

Report clusters, not only the aggregate

A line saying share of voice rose four points hides that every implementation question was lost while every comparison was won. The cluster table is what converts a number into a brief.

  • Eight rows, one per intent cluster, with the three rates.
  • Flag clusters where the competitor set changed, since that can move a rate without any change on your side.
  • Mark clusters where the sample is thin, rather than rounding them into the average.

Three formats that look rigorous and say nothing

  • A composite score with no components. It cannot be audited, so it cannot be improved, so it becomes a number the team manages rather than a measurement.
  • A percentage with no denominator. Twelve percent of what, across how many prompts, on which surfaces, on what date. Without those four, the number is decoration.
  • A keyword cloud or sentiment summary. It describes language rather than visibility, and it never changes a decision.

The common thread is that all three are easy to produce and impossible to act on. A report that cannot produce a work item has failed, however polished the chart.

What to say when the number goes down

Say which component moved first. If mention fell and citation held, the pipeline stopped reaching you, which is usually an index or access change. If citation held and absorption fell, competitors published better-structured versions.

Say whether a model release coincided with the movement. A cluster of simultaneous shifts across unrelated prompts is a release, and reporting it as performance is the fastest way to lose credibility with anyone who checks.

Say what you are doing about it, in one sentence, with a date. A report that ends on a diagnosis without an owner is a status update, not a management document.

Frequently asked questions

How long should the report be?

One page plus an appendix of cluster rows. If it needs more than a page to explain the headline, the headline is not yet understood.

Should I include competitor rankings?

Yes, from the same run. A benchmark assembled from separate reports compares methods rather than brands.

What if the board wants one number?

Give one number and its three components. The composite is fine at the top of a page as long as the components are directly beneath it.
