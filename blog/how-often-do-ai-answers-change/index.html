How often do AI answers change, and why it matters for GEO

By IndexHot Team · November 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Ask the same question twice and you may get different brand lists. That instability is not a bug in your measurement, it is a property of the system, and it has three separate causes that need three separate responses. Teams that treat all of it as one thing end up rewriting pages to fix a model release.

Three causes, three symptoms

Cause What you see What it means
Sampling Same prompt, same hour, different wording or brand order The answer is unsettled; a single run is one sample
Index change Sources appear or disappear over days to weeks Retrieval is moving; freshness and crawl matter
Model release Unrelated prompts move together on one date A release happened; your baseline is invalid

The distinction matters because only one of these responds to your work. Sampling noise responds to more runs. Index movement responds to publishing and to access. A model release responds to nothing you can do, and the honest response is to re-baseline rather than to explain it.

What normal instability looks like

In a fifty-prompt set, expect a handful of prompts to be genuinely unsettled at any given time: two runs name you, one does not. That is not failure, it is a prompt where the retrieval set is thin or where two sources disagree.

The practical rule is to code the median of three runs and to flag the prompt. A prompt that returns three different brand sets is unsettled regardless of how many more runs you add, and reporting it as a percentage implies a precision it does not have.

  • Stable: three runs agree on brand presence, even if wording differs.
  • Unsettled: presence changes across runs, order may or may not change.
  • Volatile: three runs produce three different brand sets.

Volatile prompts are not useless. They usually mark the part of the category where no source has established itself, which is exactly where a specific page can win a durable slot. Stable prompts, by contrast, are usually owned by an incumbent and are expensive to move.

Why weekly reporting misleads

If a typical prompt has a five-point chance of flipping between runs, twenty prompts produce movements that look like trends and are not. A team reporting weekly sees a two-point decline, commissions a fix, and then sees a two-point recovery that was the same noise moving back.

Monthly is the shortest interval worth reporting for a fifty-prompt library, and quarterly is better for anything involving absorption, which is judgement-coded and drifts with fatigue. The exception is an active change: if you shipped a restructure last week, run weekly for three weeks to see whether it moved, then return to monthly.

The exception has a discipline attached. Announce the reason for the higher cadence in advance, and stop when the window closes. Otherwise the weekly cadence becomes permanent and the team spends its attention on noise.

Recording what makes the numbers interpretable

Four fields per row: prompt id, surface, run date, and the model or version where the interface exposes it. Six months later these are the difference between an explanation and a guess.

Fix account state too. Logged-in and logged-out answers differ because personalisation and model version differ, so a cycle run from a different state is not comparable with the previous one, even when every prompt is identical.

  • Freeze the prompt library. Reworded prompts produce a trend that describes your edits.
  • Mark releases on the chart. A vertical line at the release date prevents misattribution.
  • Keep the raw answers. Coding rules change, and recoding from responses is cheaper than re-running.

Frequently asked questions

How many runs are enough?

Three per prompt per surface for a working read. More runs reduce sampling noise but cannot resolve an unsettled retrieval set.

Should I worry when answers change?

Only when the change persists across two cycles with the same library and no release in between. Otherwise it is variance.

Does instability mean the category is open?

Often yes. Volatile prompts tend to be ones where no source has established authority, which is where entry is cheapest.
